## 2. Why the current draft fails (the author's verdict and my diagnosis)

The author read `report/report.pdf` (23 pages, built 2026-09-04) and said: "it starts out with these very in-the-weeds assumptions and stuff; I don't think this is the right way to go. I believe the analysis is likely very strong."

My diagnosis of the draft, so you do not repeat it:

1. **The executive summary is a technical abstract, not a summary.** Finding 1 defines "capture rate" and "outside enrollment" and walks the identity before the reader knows why any of it matters. A Board member needs, in order: what is being asked, the one-sentence reason, three plain supporting facts, and what would settle it. The mechanism belongs in Section 1, not on page 1.
2. **Too many numbers per sentence.** Sentences routinely carry four or five figures (522, 403–462, 83%, 121, 553, 432, 54–65%, 81–87%). A reader can hold one or two. Pick the few that carry the argument and repeat them consistently; put the rest in tables.
3. **Mechanism before meaning.** "Combined-area capture rate", "outside enrollment", "JECC", "entry ratio", "share of simulated paths" appear before their plain-English meaning. Introduce each idea with what it means for a family or a classroom, then name it.
4. **The finding is buried.** The strongest, simplest points are: (a) the district's own count of where South Boulder children live and go to school says the merged school would be over the building, not inside the range; (b) the district has not shown the calculation that turns its resident projection into its enrollment range, and every input for that calculation is already public; (c) the first count of this school year is already above the projection; (d) the decision is irreversible and the fix for over-enrollment is not free. Those four sentences should be the spine.
5. **Section headings read like a paper.** "From residents to enrollment: the missing step" is fine; "Rooms, not seats" is fine; but the sections then open with sourcing rather than with the point.
6. **Length.** 23 pages is acceptable (the previous version was 28), but the summary (pages 1–11) is still long. A shape that would work: one page executive summary in plain prose; four to six pages of summary with one exhibit each; appendices for everything else. The author said "the simpler the better, but it's okay to keep some of the more sophisticated analysis too."

What is **good** in the draft and should survive: the evidence standard (Appendix A); the claim-by-claim table (Appendix B, trimmed); the worksheet table (Section 6); the bridge table and figure (Section 1); the Aug 2026 count figure; the sections-vs-rooms figure; the two-assumption merged-school figure; the honest concessions and caveats the QA rounds forced in.
